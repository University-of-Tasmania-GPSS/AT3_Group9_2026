## Spatial Correlation 

The spatial analysis revealed clear differences in the distribution of recorded crime across Greater Sydney LGAs. Although southern and western LGAs generally recorded higher crime counts, Sydney LGA had the highest crime rates per 1,000 residents across all three crime categories. This highlights the importance of accounting for population size when comparing crime between LGAs, as high incident counts do not necessarily correspond to the highest population-adjusted rates. The concentration of bars, clubs and social events in Sydney CBD may contribute to the high recorded crime rates, as these activities attract large numbers of visitors who are not necessarily represented in resident population figures. Jochelson (1997) similarly identified concentrations of assaults and robberies around busy commercial streets, entertainment venues, licensed premises and transport facilities in inner Sydney. The report also highlighted that transient populations can influence crime rates calculated using resident population figures, emphasising the importance of considering population movement when interpreting crime rates. However, as Jochelson's research examined incidents recorded between 1995 and 1996, it provides historical context rather than direct evidence explaining the contemporary crime patterns identified in this study.

The hotspot analysis identified spatial clustering that varied between crime categories. Woollahra was identified as a hotspot across all three categories, while Randwick was a hotspot for drug and property offences and Fairfield for violent crime. Conversely, several north-eastern LGAs, including Northern Beaches, Ku-ring-gai and Willoughby, were identified as coldspots. These patterns indicate that crime rates are not distributed evenly across the study area and that the locations of high and low crime rates differ between crime categories. However, the presence of a hotspot does not establish the underlying causes of crime in that location. Clancey (2015) highlights the importance of understanding crime prevention within its local context. In a case study of Glebe, inner Sydney, Clancey documented a combination of social programs, environmental interventions and security technologies operating within a relatively small geographical area. However, many individual initiatives had not been thoroughly evaluated, making their effectiveness difficult to establish. The study also highlighted the potential for different crime prevention initiatives to have complementary or collective effects. This illustrates why the hotspots and coldspots identified in this study should not be interpreted as evidence of particular causes or as a direct measure of crime prevention effectiveness. Consequently, the hotspot patterns should be interpreted alongside the limitations of using LGAs as the unit of spatial analysis.

The Global Getis–Ord G results provided further evidence of overall spatial concentration for drug offences and violent crime, both of which were statistically significant at the 0.05 level. Property offences did not demonstrate statistically significant global spatial concentration. These findings suggest that the degree of overall spatial concentration differs between crime categories, reinforcing the value of examining each category separately. The temporal analysis also revealed different trends between LGAs and crime types, with violent crime generally increasing while property offences generally decreased across much of the study area.

Previous research provides further context for interpreting these spatial patterns. Jochelson (1997) demonstrated the importance of examining crime at a spatial scale that captures local differences, identifying concentrations of assaults and robberies in particular locations within inner Sydney. This suggests that different patterns may be observed if this study used suburbs instead of LGAs as the unit of spatial analysis. Cameron (2022) found an association between the spatial distribution of alcohol outlets and crime, highlighting how land use and patterns of activity may contribute to differences in crime between locations. Similarly, Bright et al. (2025), using metropolitan Sydney crime data, found that the geographical dispersion of crime events varied according to offending patterns and crime type. Together, these studies suggest that crime distributions may reflect a combination of social, environmental and behavioural factors rather than socioeconomic conditions alone. However, these studies do not establish the specific causes of the spatial patterns observed in this project.

An important consideration when interpreting these results is that recorded crime does not necessarily represent the true distribution of all criminal activity. The spatial patterns may be influenced by differences in police presence, enforcement activity and the likelihood that offences are reported and recorded. Areas with greater police activity may have more offences detected, particularly for offences such as drug possession, which may be identified through proactive policing rather than public reporting. Consequently, differences in recorded crime between LGAs may partly reflect differences in detection and reporting practices. However, police deployment, reporting behaviour and detection rates were not directly examined in this project, so their influence on the observed patterns cannot be established from the results.

Furthermore, the location where an offence occurs does not necessarily correspond to the offender's or victim's residential location. People may travel between LGAs for work, entertainment and other activities, meaning that crime recorded in areas such as Sydney CBD may involve visitors and commuters rather than local residents. As crime rates in this study were calculated using resident population figures, areas with substantial daytime or evening visitor populations may have higher recorded rates relative to their resident populations. Jochelson (1997) identified this issue when examining crime in inner Sydney, reinforcing the importance of considering population movement when interpreting population-based crime rates. Further research incorporating information about offender and victim residence, incident locations and population movement could help clarify these differences.

Overall, the results demonstrate that crime patterns vary geographically, between crime categories and over time. Although the observed hotspots and crime trends provide insight into the spatial distribution of crime across Greater Sydney, further investigation is required to establish whether socioeconomic characteristics, including household income, unemployment and educational attainment, are associated with these patterns. Future research could incorporate additional variables, such as land use, the distribution of licensed venues, police deployment, reporting patterns and population movement, to investigate why some LGAs record higher crime rates than others. Clancey (2015) highlights the complexity of understanding local crime prevention, demonstrating that multiple social and environmental initiatives can operate together within a single area. This reinforces the need to consider local characteristics and crime prevention activities when interpreting spatial crime patterns, although their influence on the patterns identified in this study remains untested.

<strong>Important Note:</strong>

Correlation does not automatically imply causation. The results produced here cannot definatvely prove that these socioeconomic factors cause crime, only that there is a statistical relationship between the socioeconomic factors and the crime rates.

It is worth mentioning that the crime rates discussed here are for crimes committed in that LGA whilst the socioeconomic factors are for people living in the LGA. As discussed above, some LGA’s will have significant number of non-residents who pass through who may not live there. As previously discussed, the Sydney LGA has the highest crime rates across all three crime types. When correlation coefficients were calculated for each crime type against each socioeconomic factor. Four out of the nine relationships did not have a statistically significant correlation, however examination of the graphs produced suggested that this was because of the Sydney LGA acting as an outlier and skewing the correlation results. As such the correlation analysis was repeated without the Sydney LGA. Without the Sydney LGA the correlation values of all the relationships explored increased and three of them became statistically significant. The only relationship without a statistically significant correlation was year 12 completion and drug offences. This is unusual however as most studies conducted across the western world show that educational attainment is heavily correlated with substance abuse. For example, a study conducted in the United States showed that people who did not complete high school had much higher rates of substance abuse (Ford et al., 2020). 

Median weekly income for the LGA was negatively correlated with all three crime types, meaning that as the median weekly income of an LGA increased, it's violent crime, drug offences and property offences decreased. The unemployment level in an LGA was positively correlated with all three crime types, meaning that as the unemployment level in an LGA increased, it's violent crime, drug offences and property offences increased. The year 12 completion levels of an LGA was negatively correlated with violent crime and property offences, meaning that as the year 12 completion levels in an LGA increased, these crime types decreased. 

Crime rates are influenced by a wide range of factors. Socioeconomic conditions contribute to poverty which contributes to crime. A report by Jason Vargas (Northwestern University) states that financial strain is the major impactor in crime rates (2023). Vargas discusses the economic deprivation theory which theorises that a relationship between a lack of educational and economic resources can cause people to turn to crime as a way to grow their opportunities. The economic deprivation theory also states that individuals can become disenfranchised with the systems which they perceive as having failed to provide for them and therefore have less respect or care for the rules dictated by that system. Our analysis found correlation between most crime types and our socioeconomic factors. Whilst our analysis cannot prove causation, it aligns with other studies which have shown a causality link between these socioeconomic factors and crime rates. 

A report by Don Weatherburn for the NSW Bureau of Crime Statistics and Research discusses that these socioeconomic factors may not directly cause an individual to commit crime but instead makes them more likely to be in a circumstance where they are influenced to commit crime (2001). The example used by Weatherburn is that economic stress can cause a breakdown of the relationship between parents and children which lead children to associate with what Weatherburn describes as “delinquent peers”.
