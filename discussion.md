## Spatial Correlation 

The spatial analysis revealed clear differences in the distribution of recorded crime across Greater Sydney LGAs. Although southern and western LGAs generally recorded higher crime counts, Sydney LGA had the highest crime rates per 1,000 residents across all three crime categories. This highlights the importance of accounting for population size when comparing crime between LGAs, as high incident counts do not necessarily correspond to the highest population-adjusted rates. The concentration of bars, clubs and social events in Sydney CBD may contribute to the high recorded crime rates, as these activities attract large numbers of visitors who are not necessarily represented in resident population figures. Jochelson (1997) similarly identified concentrations of assaults and robberies around busy commercial streets, entertainment venues, licensed premises and transport facilities in inner Sydney. The report also highlighted that transient populations can influence crime rates calculated using resident population figures, emphasising the importance of considering population movement when interpreting crime rates. However, as Jochelson's research examined incidents recorded between 1995 and 1996, it provides historical context rather than direct evidence explaining the contemporary crime patterns identified in this study.

The hotspot analysis identified spatial clustering that varied between crime categories. Woollahra was identified as a hotspot across all three categories, while Randwick was a hotspot for drug and property offences and Fairfield for violent crime. Conversely, several north-eastern LGAs, including Northern Beaches, Ku-ring-gai and Willoughby, were identified as coldspots. These patterns indicate that crime rates are not distributed evenly across the study area and that the locations of high and low crime rates differ between crime categories. However, the presence of a hotspot does not establish the underlying causes of crime in that location. Clancey (2015) highlights the importance of understanding crime prevention within its local context. In a case study of Glebe, inner Sydney, Clancey documented a combination of social programs, environmental interventions and security technologies operating within a relatively small geographical area. However, many individual initiatives had not been thoroughly evaluated, making their effectiveness difficult to establish. The study also highlighted the potential for different crime prevention initiatives to have complementary or collective effects. This illustrates why the hotspots identified in this study should not be interpreted as evidence of particular causes or as a direct measure of crime prevention effectiveness. Consequently, the hotspot patterns should be interpreted alongside the limitations of using LGAs as the unit of spatial analysis.

The Global Getis–Ord G results provided further evidence of overall spatial concentration for drug offences and violent crime, both of which were statistically significant at the 0.05 level. Property offences did not demonstrate statistically significant global spatial concentration. These findings suggest that the degree of overall spatial concentration differs between crime categories, reinforcing the value of examining each category separately. The temporal analysis also revealed different trends between LGAs and crime types, with violent crime generally increasing while property offences generally decreased across much of the study area.

Previous research provides further context for interpreting these spatial patterns. Jochelson (1997) demonstrated the importance of examining crime at a spatial scale that captures local differences, identifying concentrations of assaults and robberies in particular locations within inner Sydney. Cameron (2022) found an association between the spatial distribution of alcohol outlets and crime, highlighting how land use and patterns of activity may contribute to differences in crime between locations. Similarly, Bright et al. (2025), using metropolitan Sydney crime data, found that the geographical dispersion of crime events varied according to offending patterns and crime type. Together, these studies suggest that crime distributions may reflect a combination of social, environmental and behavioural factors rather than socioeconomic conditions alone. However, these studies do not establish the specific causes of the spatial patterns observed in this project.

An important consideration when interpreting these results is that recorded crime does not necessarily represent the true distribution of all criminal activity. The spatial patterns may be influenced by differences in police presence, enforcement activity and the likelihood that offences are reported and recorded. Areas with greater police activity may have more offences detected, particularly for offences such as drug possession, which may be identified through proactive policing rather than public reporting. Consequently, differences in recorded crime between LGAs may partly reflect differences in detection and reporting practices. However, police deployment, reporting behaviour and detection rates were not directly examined in this project, so their influence on the observed patterns cannot be established from the results.

Furthermore, the location where an offence occurs does not necessarily correspond to the offender's or victim's residential location. People may travel between LGAs for work, entertainment and other activities, meaning that crime recorded in areas such as Sydney CBD may involve visitors and commuters rather than local residents. As crime rates in this study were calculated using resident population figures, areas with substantial daytime or evening visitor populations may have higher recorded rates relative to their resident populations. Jochelson (1997) identified this issue when examining crime in inner Sydney, reinforcing the importance of considering population movement when interpreting population-based crime rates. Further research incorporating information about offender and victim residence, incident locations and population movement could help clarify these differences.

Overall, the results demonstrate that crime patterns vary geographically, between crime categories and over time. Although the observed hotspots and crime trends provide insight into the spatial distribution of crime across Greater Sydney, further investigation is required to establish whether socioeconomic characteristics, including household income, unemployment and educational attainment, are associated with these patterns. Future research could incorporate additional variables, such as land use, the distribution of licensed venues, police deployment, reporting patterns and population movement, to investigate why some LGAs record higher crime rates than others. Clancey (2015) highlights the complexity of understanding local crime prevention, demonstrating that multiple social and environmental initiatives can operate together within a single area. This reinforces the need to consider local characteristics and crime prevention activities when interpreting spatial crime patterns, although their influence on the patterns identified in this study remains untested.
